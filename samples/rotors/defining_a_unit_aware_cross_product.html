Defining a Unit-Aware Cross Product

Investigation into some subtleties regarding units of measurement in the cross product. This leads to a rabbit hole with unit vectors and normal vectors and more .

Introduction

Implementing the cross product in Pacioli raised some questions that lead to a rabbit hole.

The cross product, as any other product, is not a closed product when units of measurement are considered. Gibb's original defintion is however not clear about it. A dimensioned cross vectors leads to questions about what a unit vector is and how a normal vector is computed.

This note first discusses unit vectors. Then it looks at the cross product. We look at some use cases to see the needs for uom support. We introduce a symmetric closed variant of the cross product that creates a vector perpendicular to two given vectors. The given vectors and the outcome have the same units.

The Cross Product

To implement the cross product we start from Gibb's definition.

Definition (Gibbs)

The cross product \(\v\times\w\) between vectors \(\v\) and \(\w\) is give by \[ \v\times\w = \Vert \v \Vert \Vert \w \Vert \sin(\theta)\,\mathbf{n} \]

where

  • \(\theta\) is the angle between vectors \(\v\) and \(\w\)
  • \(\mathbf{n}\) is a unit vector perpendicular to \(\v\) and \(\w\)

This form is not easily implemented, because a question is how \(\mathbf{n}\) is obtained. A common way to compute a vector perpendicular to two other vectors is to use the cross product. How do we compute \(\mathbf{n}\) if we don't have the cross product?

Fortunately, later Gibbs uses unit vectors \(\mathbf{i}\), \(\mathbf{j}\), and \(\mathbf{k}\) to derive from \( \v = v_1\mathbf{i} + v_2\mathbf{j} + v_3\mathbf{k} \) and \( \w = w_1\mathbf{i} + w_2\mathbf{j} + w_3\mathbf{k} \) that \[ \v\times\w = (v_2w_3 - v_3w_2)\mathbf{i} + (v_3w_1 - v_1w_3)\mathbf{j} + (v_1w_2 - v_2w_1)\mathbf{k} \] This can easily be implemented without \(\mathbf{n}\).

Gibb's definitions work for dimensionless vectors, but for dimensioned vectors we need some care.

We expect the type of the cross product to give the product of the vector's units. Let's write \(\text{Vec}\ a\) for the type of a vector with unit of measurement \(a\). The cross product between vectors with units \(a\) and \(b\) is typed as \[ \text{Vec}\ a \times \text{Vec}\ b \to \text{Vec}\ ab \] For example when the unit of the vectors is in metre, the cross vector magnitude is metre squared. This matches with the idea that the magnitude of the cross vector is usualy explained as the are of the parallelogram. Other confirmation is several products in physics, e.g. ... .... Just as other products, the unit of the cross product is the product of the units.

It is not directly clear how to derive such a dimensionally correct implementation of the cross product from Gibb's definition and alternative. We will see that the following version of the alternative computation gives the correct result. \[ \v \times \w = \left[ {\begin{array}{c} \v_2\w_3 - \v_3\w_2 \\ \v_3\w_1 - \v_1\w_3 \\ \v_1\w_2 - \v_2\w_1 \\ \end{array} } \right] \] However, that requires examining some details about unit and normal vectors. We will see it this depends on the subtle distinction between vector elements \(\v_i\) and \(\w_i\) and coordinates \(v_i\) and \(w_i\).

Vectors and Coordinates

The one-to-one correspondence between a vector and its coordinates is a fundamental principle in linear algebra notation. For a vector \(\v\) given as a linear combination of basis vectors \(\i\), \(\j\) and \(\k\) \[ \v = v_1\mathbf{i} + v_2\mathbf{j} + v_3\mathbf{k} \] the tuple \( (v_1, v_2, v_3) \) is called the coordinate vector. The vector and the coordinate tuple are considered the same. Gibbs writes

By means of these vectors \(\i\), \(\j\), \(\k\), such a correspondence is established between vector analysis and the analysis in Cartesian coordinates that it becomes possible to pass at will from one to the other.
Strang writes
The vector \(\v\) is identified with the point whose coordinates are \(v_1, v_2, v_3\)
and mentions this was Descartes's idea when he united geometry and algebra. This identification allows calculating with vectors via its representation. Operations are defined on coordinate vectors that translate to the represented vectors. This one-to-one translation is a basic principle.

The one-to-one correspondence requires some scrutiny when moving from dimensionless to dimensioned linear algebra. Identifying a vector with its coordinates works fine for dimensionless vector, but if we now consider a dimensioned vector we have to be careful with the units. Consider vector \( \v = \i + 2\j + 3\k \) in a dimensioned vector space with unit metre. In this space the vector elements have a unit of measurement: \[ \v = \left[ {\begin{array}{c} 1\, \text{m} \\ 2\, \text{m} \\ 3\, \text{m} \\ \end{array} } \right] \] If we look at \(\v\)'s elements we see for example that \(\v_1\) equals \(1\, \text{m}\). However, since \(\v\) and \(\i\) are both from the dimensioned space and thus have unit metre, it follows from (1) that coordinate \(v_1\) cannot have a unit. It is the undimensioned scalar 1. We see that \(\v_1\) differs from \(v_1\).

This difference is the basis of Hart's theory of dimensioned matrices. Hart p.62 a vector is a n-tuple of dimensioned scalars. By adding the units you get the rich structure of dimensioned linear algebra.

Handling this difference requires some care to avoid ambiguities.

What is a Unit Vector?

One thing that requires attention when moving to dimensioned vector spaces is the notion of a unit vector. In many applications it makes a difference whether the unit vector is dimensionless or not. [Hier iets over rotor en rotee?]

A common ambituity in mathematical texts is the unit of measurement of a unit vector. Consider the following two statements:

  • A unit vector is a vector with norm one
  • A unit vector is computed as \[\frac{\v }{\Vert \v \Vert} \nonumber \]
These two statements give a conflict when units of measurement are considered. In a normed vector space with unit of measurement metre, does a unit vector have norm 1 or norm 1m?

If the norm \(\Vert \v \Vert\) has unit metre, then it cancels \(\v\)'s unit when it is divided by \( \Vert \v \Vert\) and the result is a dimensionless unit vector. If norm \(\Vert \v \Vert\) is dimensionless it does not cancel \(\v \)'s unit and the result is a dimensioned vector with unit metre. Either way we have a contradiction.

An alternative viewpoint is that a unit vector can be obtained from a vector in two ways. When vector \(\v\) is split into scalar \(c\) and unit vector \(\mathbf{u}\) such that \(\v = c\mathbf{u}\) and \( \mathbf{u} \) has length one, then we can couple the unit with the scalar or with the unit vector, but not with both. We have to pick one.

Dimensioned and dimensionless unit vector both have use cases. For example, when a vector is written as a linear combination of unit vectors \(\mathbf{i}\), \(\mathbf{j}\), and \(\mathbf{k}\) \[ \v = v_1\mathbf{i} + v_2\mathbf{j} + v_3\mathbf{k} \nonumber \] the units are on the unit vectors to give them the same unit as \(\v\). But when vector \(\v\) is split as \(\v = \mathbf{v_{\parallel}} + \mathbf{v_{\perp}}\) with the vector's projection \[ \mathbf{v_{\parallel}} = (\v\cdot \mathbf{u})\, \mathbf{u} \nonumber \] and the vector's rejection \[ \mathbf{v_{\perp}} = -\mathbf{u}\times(\mathbf{u}\times \v) \nonumber \] the unit vector \(\mathbf{u}\) has to be dimensionless to give \(\mathbf{v_{\parallel}}\) and \(\mathbf{v_{\perp}}\) the same unit as \(\v\).

Unit Vectors in Pacioli

Since Pacioli supports units of measurement we have to be careful to use the right unit vector. For a vector \(\v\), function normalized creates a vector in the same direction as \(\v\) with the same units but with norm one. 

        declare normalized :: for_index P: for_unit a: (a*P!) -> a*P!;

        define normalized(x) = x '/.' magnitude(norm(x));
To get a dimensionless unit vector we can do 
        magnitude(normalized(v))
or directly 
        v '/.' norm(v)
Both cases give a dimensionless unit vector, since function norm gives a dimensioned number.

Deriving the Dimensioned Cross Product

Gibb's uses the following propeties of the base vectors when deriving the alternative computation.

  • \( \i \times \i = \j \times \j = \k \times \k = 0 \)
  • \( \i \times \j = -\j \times \i = \k \)
  • \( \j \times \k = -\k \times \j = \i \)
  • \( \k \times \i = -\i \times \k = \j \)
These clearly only work in the dimensionless case.

Using furthermore the distributivity of the cross product he derives $$ \begin{eqnarray*} \v \times \w &=& (v_1\mathbf{i} + v_2\mathbf{j} + v_3\mathbf{k}) \times (w_1\mathbf{i} + w_2\mathbf{j} + w_3\mathbf{k}) \\ &=& v_1w_1\mathbf{i}\times\mathbf{i} + v_1w_2\mathbf{i}\times\mathbf{j} + v_1w_3\mathbf{i}\times\mathbf{k}\ + \\ & & v_2w_1\mathbf{j}\times\mathbf{i} + v_2w_2\mathbf{j}\times\mathbf{j} + v_2w_3\mathbf{j}\times\mathbf{k}\ + \\ & & v_3w_1\mathbf{k}\times\mathbf{i} + v_3w_2\mathbf{k}\times\mathbf{j} + v_3w_3\mathbf{k}\times\mathbf{k} \\ &=& (v_2w_3 - v_3w_2)\mathbf{i} + (v_3w_1 - v_1w_3)\mathbf{j} + (v_1w_2 - v_2w_1)\mathbf{k} \end{eqnarray*} $$ Gibb's text does only work for dimensionless vectors

We can simply add step \[ \v \times \w = \left[ {\begin{array}{c} \v_2\w_3 - \v_3\w_2 \\ \v_3\w_1 - \v_1\w_3 \\ \v_1\w_2 - \v_2\w_1 \\ \end{array} } \right] \] The alternative (2) is less clear. At first glance it seems to give a vector from the same space. This would be a closed cross product. A cross product of type \(\text{Vec}\ a \times \text{Vec}\ a \to \text{Vec}\ a \). However, this depends on how the units are attached to the coordinates \(v_i\) or the base vectors \(\i\), \(\j\) and \(\k\).

If we look at Gibb's definition we can arrive at the correct units. The unit of a vector's norm \(\Vert \v \Vert\) equals the vector's unit. This means that definition (1) gives the correct product of the units as long as unit vector \(\mathbf{n}\) is dimensionless.

Cross Product in Pacioli

We define the cross product as follows in Pacioli 

    declare cross ::
        for_unit a, b: (a*Geom3!, b*Geom3!) -> a*b*Geom3!;

    define cross(v, w) =
        let
            (vx, vy, vz) = values3d(v),
            (wx, wy, wz) = values3d(w)
        in
            vector3d(vy*wz - vz*wy, vz*wx - vx*wz, vx*wy - vy*wx)
        end;
Here a*Geom3! is Pacioli's notation for \( \text{Vec}\ a\). 
    vector3d :: for_unit a: (a, a, a) -> a*Geom3!
    values3d :: for_unit a: (a*Geom3!) -> Tuple(a, a, a)

in our notation this would be \[ a \times a \times a \to \text{Vec}\ a \nonumber \] \[ \text{Vec}\ a \to a \times a \times a \nonumber \] The product of the units seems okay, since the norm of the cross products represents an area. So for vectors from a metre space it gives unit metre squared.

So the type is \( \text{Vec}\ a \times \text{Vec}\ b \to \text{Vec}\ ab \), as expected.

With this version of the cross vector we can actually implement Gibb's original definition, since we only need a dimensionless normal. With the help of some utilities we can define: 


    define dimless_normal(v, w) = 
        let x = cross(v, w) in x '/.' norm(x) end;
    
    define cross_def(v, w) =
        norm(v) * norm(w) * sin(angle_between_vectors(v, w)) '.*' dimless_normal(v, w);
The types are as expected: 
    dimless_normal :: for_unit b, a: (a*Geom3!, b*Geom3!) -> Geom3!
    cross_def :: for_unit b, a: (a*Geom3!, b*Geom3!) -> a*b*Geom3!;

The cross vectors turns out to be no problem, but the question remains how to compute a normal vector. The cross product gives a vector in the wrong space.

Conclusion

Units of measurement makes the definition of the cross product a little more subtle. Following Gibbs and creating a closed cross product in Pacioli would lead to: 
        cross(v, w) = magnitude(norm(v)) * magnitude(norm(w)) * sin(angle(v, w)) * normalized(perpendicular(v, w))
This gives a closed cross product with correct units of measurement. The magnitude around the norms are needed to cancel the unit from the norms. The unit comes from the normalized(perpendicular(v, w)) term.

As long as we stay in the dimensionless world it does not matter. We can use this version of the cross product to do physics etc. But as soon as units are added, we need to distinguish closed and non-closed operations.

We prefer the non-closed cross product. Function perpendicular is given as a closed variant.
